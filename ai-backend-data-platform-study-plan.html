AI-driven Backend Developer (Python/TypeScript), Data Platform - platform layer for agentic apps

6-week plan plus an optional add-on week for a senior backend role building the platform that replaces a third-party CRM. Weekdays ~2-3 h, weekends one long block for the workbook exercises. Checkboxes save in this browser.

Overview

The client, an enterprise data and AI platform company, is moving its own business apps off a major third-party CRM onto systems it builds. This role builds the platform layer underneath: the architecture and service boundaries, the PostgreSQL data models, the Python and TypeScript services, the APIs, events and workflow primitives that product teams and agentic apps build on, and the migration path off the CRM. Early months are whiteboard-heavy. On-site in Mountain View, CA, 6-12 months, via Toptal.

Honest fit note. This is a senior backend engineering role first. Your infrastructure depth covers one required bullet fully and makes you unusually strong on "running services reliably in production", but the interviewers will probe application backend depth: designing a schema for a complex business domain, explaining a query plan, choosing between a REST and an event interface, and defending a service boundary. Nothing on record shows that yet, so you prove it with a built system and a confident whiteboard session. Pitch yourself as a backend engineer with an operator's instincts, not as a DevOps engineer who also codes. Full role track: study/roles/07-ai-backend-data-platform/highlights.md.

Portfolio: an "own your CRM" platform

A public repo, synthetic data only, that rebuilds a thin slice of what the client is building:

  • Data: PostgreSQL schema for accounts, contacts, leads, opportunities and activities, multi-tenant with row-level security, activities partitioned by month, an append-only audit log
  • Services: a Python FastAPI service that owns accounts and contacts, a TypeScript Fastify service that owns opportunities; each owns its tables and they talk through APIs and events
  • Events: a transactional outbox in each service, relayed to Kafka (Redpanda locally) by a small Go service; idempotent consumers
  • Workflows: Temporal lead assignment with manager approval and opportunity stage sign-off
  • Agent surface: an MCP server with task-level tools; reads scoped by RLS, writes through a Temporal approval, every call in the audit log
  • Migration: a Salesforce-shaped CSV importer keeping 18-character legacy ids, with a reconciliation report
  • Delivery and docs: Docker Compose, a Helm chart, CI with Testcontainers, migration lint, a load test; one ADR per major decision and docs/query-plans.md

If time runs short, cut the GraphQL layer, then the Go relay (use a Python poller), then the Helm chart. Do not cut RLS, the outbox, the Temporal approval or the reconciliation report: those are the interview stories.

Week 2 - PostgreSQL in depth

"Deep database experience, especially PostgreSQL" is a required bullet, and you know Postgres as infrastructure. This week makes you the schema designer: constraints as business rules, indexes from queries, reading plans, tenancy enforced by RLS, and the locking behavior that decides whether a migration is safe. The workbook runs real PostgreSQL 18 in-process through PGlite.

MonModeling: keys, constraints, custom fields, soft deletes

Normalize first, then denormalize on purpose. Surrogate keys (UUIDv7 or bigint identity) plus the legacy CRM id, foreign keys, CHECK and UNIQUE as business rules, jsonb for genuinely open-ended custom fields, and why soft deletes complicate uniqueness.

Tasks

  • Read the JSON types page (jsonb operators, containment, GIN opclasses)
  • Model accounts, contacts, opportunities and activities on paper with every constraint you can justify; mark which CRM validation rules became constraints
  • Write the partial unique index for contact email that ignores soft-deleted rows; explain why a plain UNIQUE breaks
  • Decide enums vs lookup tables for opportunity stage and defend it in two sentences

Materials

TueIndexes that match the queries

B-tree column order, composite and covering (INCLUDE) indexes, partial and expression indexes, GIN for jsonb, and indexes that support keyset pagination. Design indexes from the query, not from the table.

Week 5 - Events, the outbox and Go

Events are how Keystone services integrate without sharing tables. This week is about correctness, not throughput: no dual writes, at-least-once delivery handled by idempotent consumers, and Go for the platform glue.

MonThe Kafka model, as an application developer

Topics, partitions, keys and per-key ordering (key by account id), consumer groups, offsets, retention, compaction, delivery semantics and the idempotent producer. Kinesis shards map to partitions and checkpoints to committed offsets; what is new is writing correct producers and consumers.

Tasks

  • Read the Kafka introduction and the message delivery semantics section
  • Run Redpanda locally (one container) and produce/consume keyed messages with rpk
  • Write down what "exactly once" means inside Kafka and why it does not extend to your database
  • Map your Kinesis/Firehose knowledge onto Kafka terms in a two-column table

Materials

  • Kafka introduction
  • Kafka docs: message delivery semantics

TueDual writes and the transactional outbox

Writing to the database and publishing to Kafka are two systems and one of them will fail. Write the event row in the same transaction as the change; relay it by polling or by CDC (Debezium outbox router).

Tasks

  • Read the transactional outbox pattern and the Debezium outbox event router

WedIdempotent consumers, DLQs and event design

Consumers are at-least-once, so make them idempotent: a processed-event table in the same transaction as the effect, or natural idempotency. Dead-letter topics, poison messages, replay. Thin notification vs full-state events, schema evolution, versioned event types.
